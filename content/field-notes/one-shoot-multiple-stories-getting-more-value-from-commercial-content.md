"We need photos and some video for the website and social media."

That's a useful starting point, but it leaves quite a few decisions open.

What needs to appear on the website? What should someone understand from the video? Are we introducing the business, showing a product, explaining a service, or promoting something specific?

A commercial shoot becomes much more useful when we answer those questions before we arrive.

Getting more value doesn't mean asking one production day to capture everything imaginable. It means planning a manageable set of stories and making sure the finished content has a clear place to go.

## Start with the customer's decision

Imagine a restaurant planning new content. This is an example, not a description of a particular client.

A potential guest may want to understand the atmosphere, the food, and what kind of visit to expect. The restaurant may need a wide image for a website header, photographs of selected dishes, a short video introducing the space, and several vertical pieces for social use.

Those needs overlap, but they aren't identical.

Now consider a builder. A prospective client may need to see the finished project, understand the workmanship, and hear how the builder approaches the process. The shot list should look different because the customer's questions are different.

Start there. What does someone need to see or understand before taking the next step with your business?

## Separate the stories before combining the production

A business introduction, a product feature, and an owner interview can all belong to the same marketing plan. They still need their own purpose.

For each piece, decide who it is for, what it needs to communicate, where it will appear, and what action should follow. Then look for opportunities to produce them efficiently together.

For example, the same prepared environment might support both still photography and video. But a clean interview recording may require a quieter time, a different setup, and people who are available to participate.

Planning that in advance helps us make sensible choices. It also helps you see where an additional session would be more practical than overloading the first one.

## Plan for the shape of the final content

A wide website banner and a vertical reel do not use the frame in the same way.

We can sometimes capture material with several crops in mind, but that takes intention. A composition built around an important detail at the edge may not survive a vertical crop. A website image may need open space for a headline that a social post doesn't need.

Video adds more choices: spoken audio, captions, on-screen text, music, length, and where the opening and ending belong.

Tell us about those uses while we're planning. "We'll crop it later" can work for some material, but it shouldn't be the entire distribution strategy.

## Preparation protects the time you have booked

Before the shoot, confirm what needs to be ready and who needs to be there.

For a restaurant, that may include the selected dishes, presentation details, staff availability, and a plan that doesn't conflict with normal service. For a business location, it may involve cleaning, access, signage, products, and permission from anyone appearing on camera.

Choose the priorities. If a particular image is essential for the website launch, it should not be competing with ten unplanned ideas at the end of the day.

Also agree on the deliverables, editing, revisions, and intended usage. More concepts, more people, or additional versions can change the production scope. A clear brief makes the budget more understandable and the day easier to run.

## The people can be a separate story worth telling

Sometimes the location and product footage answer one set of questions while the owner or team can answer another.

Why was the business created? What should a first-time customer know? What does the team pay attention to that may not be obvious in the finished result?

Those conversations can be recorded on location when that makes sense. When the business is busy, noisy, or difficult to control, a session at [Creator Studios in Bohemia](https://www.licreatorstudios.com/) can give the interview its own space and attention.

It is a complementary option, not a reason to force a studio session into every project.

## Ask for a useful set, not the biggest folder

Before the shoot, make a simple list of where the content will go. After delivery, assign each finished piece to those uses and keep the relevant permissions with the files.

A smaller set that answers real customer questions can be more useful than a large folder nobody knows how to publish.
