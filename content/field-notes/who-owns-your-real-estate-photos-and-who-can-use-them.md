You've paid for a property photo shoot. The images arrive, they look great, and the builder asks whether you can send them over for the company website.

It feels like a simple request. Everyone worked on the same property, so why wouldn't everyone be able to use the photographs?

The answer comes down to the difference between receiving the finished files and having permission to use them for a particular purpose.

A photography license sets out the allowed uses. It doesn't necessarily give the person booking the shoot ownership of the copyright or permission to pass usage rights to other businesses.

This isn't about making a friendly request difficult. It's about making sure the agreement matches the people and purposes involved.

## Who owns the copyright in a photograph?

Under U.S. copyright law, the photographer is generally the initial author and copyright owner. Work-made-for-hire arrangements and written transfers can change ownership. Paying for a commissioned shoot does not, by itself, mean the client receives the copyright. The [U.S. Copyright Office explains these distinctions](https://www.copyright.gov/engage/photographers/).

For Photografik projects, our [published terms and conditions](/terms) distinguish company ownership from the client's usage license. The terms also explain that raw materials are not included unless separately purchased or licensed.

Your written project agreement matters. Read it alongside the applicable terms rather than assuming every photographer or every type of booking uses the same arrangement.

## What does a usage license actually tell you?

A useful licensing conversation answers practical questions: who will use the media, where it will appear, what it will promote, and how long the permission applies.

For a listing, the intended use may be marketing a particular property. A designer's website portfolio is a different purpose. A manufacturer's advertising campaign is another one again.

The fact that the same photograph could work for all three doesn't make those uses interchangeable.

It helps to think of licensing as part of the brief, not something added after production. When the intended uses are clear at the beginning, we can discuss the right scope before anyone starts sharing the files.

## Can the builder, designer, or homeowner use the listing photos?

Don't assume that someone else's license covers them.

Photografik's published terms provide a limited, non-exclusive, non-transferable license for the agreed purpose after full payment. They also explain that third-party rights are not automatically included and that providing media to others requires written approval.

So when a builder or designer asks for images, introduce them to us and explain the proposed use. We can review the request and discuss the appropriate permission or separate license. That is much clearer than forwarding a download link and leaving everyone to guess.

The same principle applies to a homeowner, another agent, or a business connected to the project. Being involved with the property doesn't automatically establish the right to use the photography for a separate purpose.

## Does giving credit make the use okay?

A credit identifies the photographer. It is not a substitute for the permission needed for a use.

A useful approach is to ask first, explain the plan, and get the answer in writing. That avoids treating a tag, a forwarded email, or a conversation with someone else as if it settled the licensing question.

Likewise, the appearance of an image on a public website doesn't tell you what another person is authorized to do with it. The Copyright Office's [guidance on using photographs](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-fairuse.html) explains why ownership and permission matter, while recognizing that legal exceptions can depend on the circumstances.

## What should you tell us before booking?

Tell us about the uses you already have in mind, even when several businesses are involved.

For example, you may need listing marketing now and a separate portfolio presentation later. Or an architect and builder may both want to use the finished photographs on their own websites. A product supplier may have an advertising use that is different from either of those.

Include the proposed platforms, the businesses using the media, and any publication or campaign plans. Then we can identify what belongs in the agreement and what requires a separate discussion.

Don't worry about arriving with legal terminology. A clear description of what you want to do is a good starting point.

## Keep the paperwork with the files

When the media is delivered, keep the agreement and any later permissions in the same project folder. That makes it easier for your team to answer a request without relying on memory.

When a new use comes up, check it before publishing. A quick conversation is usually more productive than trying to sort out assumptions after a campaign is already running.
