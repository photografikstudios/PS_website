There's a big difference between saying you're easy to work with and hearing a client explain what made the experience easy.

The first is a claim. The second can give a prospective client something specific to picture.

That's why I like testimonial interviews. When they're done thoughtfully, they help answer the question someone may be asking quietly while looking at your website: "What would this actually be like for me?"

A useful client testimonial video doesn't need to make you sound perfect. It should help another person understand the client's situation, the experience of working together, and the outcome the client can honestly describe.

## Start with the client's experience, not your talking points

It's tempting to approach a testimonial with a list of things you'd like the client to say. Professional. Responsive. Great results. Highly recommended.

But that can make the interview feel like a request for compliments.

I'd rather begin with what was going on before the project. What did the client need? What were they unsure about? What mattered most when choosing someone to help?

For a real estate agent, the answer might involve preparing to sell while managing a busy schedule. For a designer, it might involve helping a homeowner make decisions that initially felt overwhelming. For a business service, it might be a problem the client didn't have time or expertise to solve alone.

The person watching can then decide whether that situation sounds familiar. The story has a reason to matter beyond praising the company.

## Ask questions that invite a real answer

I would start with four questions: What were you trying to accomplish? What were you concerned about? What stood out about the experience? What would you tell someone considering the same next step?

Then listen for something specific.

If the client says communication was great, ask what that looked like. If they say the process was easier than expected, ask which part made the difference. A useful detail often comes from the follow-up question rather than the first answer.

You don't need to script the response. Give the person time to think, let them finish, and avoid interrupting to improve the wording.

The finished video can be edited for clarity and length, but it should preserve what the client meant. A cleaner sentence isn't worth changing the substance of their experience.

## Choose someone whose story answers a real concern

Your most enthusiastic client isn't automatically the only person worth interviewing.

Think about the questions prospective clients ask before hiring you. Someone who was nervous about appearing on camera could speak to that experience. A seller who needed help understanding the preparation process could explain how the guidance felt. A business owner could describe what it took to fit a shoot into normal operations.

Different stories help different readers. You don't need one testimonial to carry every message.

And the result doesn't always have to be a dramatic number. A clearer process, less uncertainty, or a finished piece the client feels comfortable sharing can be meaningful when the client describes it honestly.

Specific sales, revenue, or audience-growth claims need records behind them. Even then, one person's result shouldn't be presented as a promise to everyone who hires you.

## Make the interview comfortable

Tell the client how the recording will work and where you would like to use it. Arrange the necessary permissions before publication, including any project images or other people who will appear.

Choose a setting that feels appropriate to the story. That might be a quiet space at the client's business or a studio where sound, lighting, and interruptions are easier to manage.

A conversation with an interviewer can be more comfortable than asking someone to stare into a lens and deliver praise. There is room to pause, repeat a thought, or clarify something.

Don't coach them into a version of events they didn't experience. The value is their perspective.

## Put the testimonial where the question comes up

A testimonial can sit beside the service it describes, appear in a relevant project story, or be shared with a prospect who has a similar concern.

A short excerpt may work on social media, while a longer version gives someone more context on your website. The edit should fit the location without stripping out important qualifications.

A written excerpt can help too, as long as it's accurate and approved. Keep a record of the original interview and the permissions associated with it.
